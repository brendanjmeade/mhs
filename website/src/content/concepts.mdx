# Singularity free homogeneous half-space triangular dislocation elements

Classical elastic dislocation theory represents a fault as a surface of zero thickness across which displacement jumps. It is enormously useful for kinematic problems.  It also has two structural challenges: 1) unphysical coincident stresses and 2) infinitely thin faults.  The mollified elastic dislocation theory used here addresses both of those challenges, providing everywhere finite stresses and a representation of finite-width faults.  The approach here is appropriate for cases where one wants meaningful element self-stresses or fault zones of extended thickness.

Following Cortez ([2001](https://doi.org/10.1137/S106482750038146X),
[Cortez et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1830486),
[Ferranti and Cortez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcp.2024.113004)), the
point force is replaced by a smooth blob of width ε,

$$
\phi_\varepsilon(r) = \frac{15\,\varepsilon^4}{8\pi}\,
\bigl(r^2 + \varepsilon^2\bigr)^{-7/2}
$$

and the elastostatic equations are then solved exactly for that body force:

$$
\mu\,\nabla^2 \mathbf{u}
+ (\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{u})
+ \mathbf{f}\,\phi_\varepsilon = \mathbf{0}
$$

The result is singular nowhere and still satisfies the Cauchy equations for static elasticity. A fault therefore has a finite width ε, and stress is defined on the fault, where classical dislocation theory returns a value ordinary integration does not define.

A half space is imposed by Mindlin's image construction. That is an image
**system** rather than a single mirrored source: reflecting the geometry is one
multiply, but what acts at the reflected point is a monopole at strength
(3−4ν), a doublet whose strength scales with the source depth, and a
logarithmic term belonging to a vertical *line* through the image. A single
mirror does not satisfy the free-surface condition in elasticity; the whole set
does, and nothing less.

## Known limitations

1. The free-surface traction condition is only satisfied to O(ε²), not exactly.
