## A shadow, not a trigger

The [on-fault page](../onfault/) solves three rough faults that break the
free surface at one load. The question behind it was whether the faults
*trigger* one another: whether A's slip brings B or C to failure before the
remote load would. Measured on that system, it does not. For each fault the
load at which it first slips is the same, to the bisection tolerance of
0.1 %, whether it is solved alone or in the system, with both P0 kernels:
the remote load reaches an element's strength before any neighbour's stress
does. What the neighbours change is *completion*. Solved alone, C has
activated 73 % of its area by 1.6 λ₀; in the system, in A's stress shadow,
28 %. A and B, which are fully active by 1.6 λ₀ either way, are behind by
under 1 % at 1.1 λ₀. The two kernels agree on every number.

That is a static result: the LCP at each load has one solution and no
history. Local, positive transfer, one element's slip bringing its
neighbours to failure, is a different thing, and it needs no second fault.

## One fault is the avalanche system

Take A alone, on the page's own mesh (3748 elements of side
h = 0.25 km once the junction constraint is gone), the same roughness,
friction 0.6 on 150 MPa, the same thrust load, and run the loading history
of the [active fractal](../fractal/) page on it: every element locked; the
load rises until one element reaches its static strength; that element
slips by exactly the amount that returns it to its dynamic strength, 10 %
lower, and re-locks; its slip changes the stress on every other element
through the interaction matrix; any element now at static strength slips
too, and so on until the fault is in equilibrium again. One event is the
difference between two equilibria. There is no time and nothing propagates;
the load is a parameter.

The interaction matrix decides what this history can do. Its branching
ratio, the stress one failure sends to the others as a fraction of the
stress it sheds, is 0.98 on A, averaged over the elements. A near-planar
crack under uniform load is critical by geometry: the interior row sums of
its self-stress operator nearly vanish, because a dislocation loop that
closes transfers everything it releases. The roughness, the rim and the
free surface make up the missing 2 %.

On A, 98 % of all element failures are transfer-triggered: of the 1.18
million failures to twice λ₀, only the 26,655 that start an event are the
load's. Every element fails earlier than the remote load alone would fail
it, by a median of 0.07 λ₀ and a 90th percentile of 0.21 λ₀. The first
event, at λ₀ itself, is the near-planar belt where the roughness tapers to
zero at the rim and the trace, 1224 elements at once, a third of the fault.
Past the transient the sizes are a power law: the maximum-likelihood
exponent of the density, fitted above ten elements, is 1.59, the largest
event covers 64 % of the fault, and events of a quarter of the fault or
more recur 224 times in the history, spaced 0.0045 λ₀ apart on average
with a coefficient of variation of 1.35, which is to say not periodically.

## The resolution ladder

Every size above is in elements. The slip of one failure is the 9 MPa
strength drop over the element's self-stiffness, and that stiffness scales
like 1/h, so an element fails more often on a finer mesh and the lower end
of every distribution is set by the mesh. The ladder runs the same fault at
h = 0.35, 0.25, 0.18 and 0.13 km with the mollification width at 0.1 h
throughout and the taper belt held at the page's 1.5 km in kilometres, not
in h, so that the fault itself does not change between rungs.

What belongs to the fault. The largest event is 63, 64, 67 and 69 % of
the fault from the coarsest rung to the finest, and the size distribution
by area is the same curve above a few square kilometres on all four. The
transfer signature per element is resolution-independent: a median advance
of 0.06 to 0.07 λ₀ and a 90th percentile of 0.21 to 0.22 λ₀ on every rung.
The number of elements that fail per unit load scales exactly like 1/h
(227, 315, 431, 597 failures per element to twice λ₀), as the stiffness
argument says it must.

What moves with the mesh. The branching ratio climbs toward 1 with
refinement, 0.968, 0.980, 0.991, 0.9986, because the row-sum deficit of a
closed loop is an edge effect that refinement removes. The exponent drifts
with it, 1.63, 1.59, 1.55, 1.51, toward the mean-field value of 1.5 that a
critical branching process has at B = 1, and it is not converged at h =
0.13: the next rung would be a 35 GB matrix. The moments scale with h and
are not fault properties at all in this model; a figure quoted in newton
metres below is the h = 0.25 cell model's. So the honest statement of the
result is: a single rough fault is a critical system whose criticality
sharpens with resolution, with events of up to two-thirds of the fault
whose area distribution is converged, and an exponent between 1.5 and
1.6 whose exact value is still the mesh's.

## Roughness and length

The roughness sets the thresholds, not the criticality. A rougher A (rms
facet slope 0.40 instead of 0.25) spreads the no-interaction failure loads
of its elements from a 95th percentile of 1.4 λ₀ to 3.5 λ₀, and fifty of
its elements can never fail under the remote load at all; the transfer
brings those misoriented facets to failure anyway, a median of 0.11 λ₀ and a
90th percentile of 0.64 λ₀ early. Its branching ratio is 0.974 against
0.980, its exponent 1.65 against 1.59, its largest event 50 % of the fault
against 64 %, and the fault-scale events fall from 224 to 66. Halving the
correlation length to 2 km sits between the two: 131 fault-scale events,
an exponent of 1.61. Roughness cuts the fault-scale events and spreads the
first failures; it barely moves the branching ratio, because that is the
geometry of a closed dislocation loop, not of its texture.

The stadium is A pulled apart along strike by an 18 km straight run, cut by
the surface like A, with the same roughness, taper and gates: 10,488
elements, 290 km², a 27 km trace. Its branching ratio is 0.991. Its
history has 70,168 events to twice λ₀, 7311 of them of a hundred elements
or more, with an exponent of 1.55, closer to the mean-field 1.5 as the
branching ratio approaches 1. The largest event is 45 % of the fault, 129
km², larger than all of A. What the stadium does not produce is
along-strike ruptures: the events of a hundred elements or more have a
median along-strike-to-down-dip aspect of 1.14, against 0.94 on the disc.
The big events are compact patches that happen to sit on a longer fault.

The first failures follow the roughness: the taper belt at λ₀, then the
facets the remote load favours, then the ones the transfer reaches. The
largest events are not the first-failing regions; they are the ones that
have re-locked and been re-loaded by the slip around them, and on every
variant they overlap the same part of the fault.

## The same map without the drop

The history has two strengths per element: it fails at the static one and
stops at the dynamic one, and that overshoot is what the neighbours feel.
The static LCP of the thresholds scan has one. Its solution at a load is
unique and path-free: every slipping element sits exactly at strength,
every other below it, and the slip is a continuous function of the load,
so there are no events to outline. What it does have is an activation
load per element, the smallest load at which its slip becomes positive on
a scan, and that is the counterpart of the first-failure map. Solved at
the static strength and at the dynamic strength, the two LCPs bracket the
history element by element: on A every one of the 3748 elements first
fails no later than it activates in the LCP at static strength and no
earlier than in the LCP at dynamic strength, and at twice λ₀ its slip lies
between the two LCPs' slips on every element.

The three maps have the same shape because the geometry and the load
decide where slip wants to be; the two thresholds decide when it arrives
and in what pieces. The history's first event takes the taper belt, 1224
elements, at λ₀ in one cascade; the static-strength LCP activates the same
elements one by one between 1.0025 and 1.045 λ₀. In moment the history
lands 46 % of the way from one LCP to the other. For a total at a given
load, either LCP is within 6 % of the history and the pair bounds it. For
the events, their sizes, their recurrence and who triggers whom, the LCP
has nothing to say: all of that is the drop's, and the drop is the one
thing it does not have.

## What this is, and is not

The moments are a cell model's. Each failure moves one element by the
drop over its self-stiffness, about 5 cm at h = 0.25 km, so an event over
64 % of A carries 1.3 × 10¹⁷ N m, a moment magnitude of 5.3, with an
event-averaged stress drop of a fraction of a megapascal; the stadium's
largest reaches 2.2 × 10¹⁷ N m. Sizes as fractions of the fault, and the
distributions by area and moment that the ladder shows to be
resolution-independent, are the quantities to quote. The exponents carry
the 5–7 % spread between update rules that the cascade solver measures
on its own fixture (sequential, random and synchronous relaxation give
different histories from the same state) on top of the drift with
resolution above. A 2 % spread in the friction changes none of the
numbers above materially (exponent 1.59, largest event 67 %); coupling the
normal stress to the slip raises the branching ratio to 0.985 and the
fault-scale count from 224 to 304, with the exponent at 1.57. On the stadium the symmetric part of the operator has a
negative eigenvalue, the long fault's body modes at this mollification
width; the event-driven history never solves that system, only its
columns, and the static solutions at the two strengths bracket every
element the history activates, on every variant.

## Reproducing it

```sh
python studies/active_fractal/onfault.py thresholds          # the opening figure
python studies/active_fractal/avalanche.py assemble A --workers 12
python studies/active_fractal/avalanche.py run A            # 3-5 s of history
python studies/active_fractal/avalanche.py run A --spread 0.02
python studies/active_fractal/avalanche.py run A --coupled
python studies/active_fractal/avalanche.py lcp A            # the static LCP's scan, ~15 min
python studies/active_fractal/avalanche.py figure            # the table, four figures
```

`assemble` is its own process because mhs parallelises over spawned
workers and a second pool after a closed one deadlocks; the matrix is
cached by the mesh's hash and `run` reads it. The variants are `A`,
`A_rough`, `A_corr2`, `stadium` and the ladder `A_h0.35`, `A_h0.18`,
`A_h0.13` (13,935 elements, an 8.7 GB matrix, 25 minutes on twelve
workers). The stadium is a spec key: `run=18.0` on any fault of
`system.build_system`, gated in `selfcheck.py -k system`; the per-event
recorder is gated in `-k slip`.
